## Hour 1 — From curiosity to a claim that can be wrong

Covers `vr.formulate` objectives 0–3. **50 minutes.**

**The vibe analogy** (carried from the August draft, still the best opener we have): you read a
room instantly — tense meeting, awkward party — without parsing every conversation. You detect the
*shape*. Models have internal states and we can read them the same way.

**Then the work.** Each student writes these five lines, in this order, on one page. **Write the
first four now.** The fifth comes after the next part.

- the curiosity, one sentence, **in their own words** — the supplied one, restated without looking
- the claim, with a **direction** — not "X relates to Y" but "X increases Y"
- what observation would **falsify** it
- **what they are hoping for**, named as a stake rather than a prediction
- **what result — either way — would let you stop**

The fourth line is the easiest to skip. A stake you have written down can be audited; a stake you
have not is just a thumb on the scale.

**Leave the fifth line blank for now.** It is the one this course added last, and it is the one an
agent will cost you.

### Falsifiable is not the same as finishable

Everything else on that page defends you against believing something false. The fifth line defends
something else entirely: **a claim can be perfectly falsifiable and still have no end.** Nothing
about it is wrong. No gate will ever fire on it. You simply work for a month and arrive nowhere,
and every individual step was defensible.

This is the failure an enthusiastic collaborator produces, and your agent is the most
enthusiastic collaborator you will ever have. It can always propose a next step. The next step
will always sound reasonable — *"we should control for that first,"* *"it would be stronger with
another condition,"* *"before we can answer this we need to establish…"* Each is good advice. In
sequence, unbounded, they are a goose chase with excellent manners.

**You cannot out-argue this in the moment.** In the moment the suggestion is genuinely sensible and
you are genuinely curious. The only defence is a stopping condition written down **before** you
start, by the version of you who was not yet invested.

> **A question with no stopping condition is not a question. It is a topic.**
> Topics are where agents take you.

### You already know how to do this

If you have built an agent, you have already done the core of this. Somewhere along the way you
decided what "working" would look like, and you checked for it. That is the same muscle. The only
difference is that almost nobody writes that check for a *question*.

That transfer is the whole reason the prerequisite exists. You are not here to learn to build an
agent. You have one. You are here to learn where to point it.

**Now write the fifth line:** what result, either way, would let you stop?

**Pair exercise, two passes.** Swap pages.

1. Find the claim that **cannot fail**.
2. Find the claim that **cannot finish**. It is harder to see, because it does not look like a
   mistake. It looks like ambition.

## Hour 2 — The prediction game, which is pre-registration in miniature

Covers `vr.formulate` 4–5. **40 minutes.**

**The game (15 min).** Three short claims about model behaviour, each taken from a published paper.
Before any reveal, every student writes down two things for each claim: **true or false**, and
**how sure**, as a number from 50% to 100%. Ninety seconds, with no agent and no search, and then
you send your answers to the facilitator. **That is pre-registration**: a prediction and a
confidence, written before the data. It costs nothing, and it changes everything about how the
reveal lands.

Then the claims are revealed one at a time. A reveal is not just *true* or *false*. It is **the one
thing that decides it**, shown from the primary source.

**Score how sure you were, not only whether you were right.** For each claim: if you were right,
your penalty is (1 − confidence)²; if you were wrong, it is confidence². The lowest total wins.

| how sure you wrote | penalty if right | penalty if wrong |
|---|---|---|
| 50% | 0.25 | 0.25 |
| 60% | 0.16 | 0.36 |
| 70% | 0.09 | 0.49 |
| 80% | 0.04 | 0.64 |
| 90% | 0.01 | 0.81 |
| 100% | 0 | 1 |

Being right still counts: a right answer never costs more than a wrong one. What the rule punishes
is being sure and wrong. One miss at 90% costs 0.81, more than three right answers at 60% put
together (0.48). On average, nothing beats writing down how sure you really are.

> **Pre-registration does not make you right. It makes your wrongness visible — to you, first.**

**"I was wrong", published (8 min).** One case, told straight. A research team wrote down what it
expected before the data came in, measured the opposite, and published it anyway, with a sentence
saying so. The point is not the humility. **The point is the second finding the falsification
bought**, which exists only because the forecast was on paper before the data arrived.

Why is "I was wrong" the most valuable sentence in research? A confirmation tells you what you
already believed. A falsification written down in advance tells you something you could not have
learned any other way, and it usually tells you what your instrument was getting wrong.

**Play it on yourself (3 min).** Go back to the one-page from hour 1. Under the claim, write **your
prediction: will your claim hold?** Add how sure you are, on the same 50–100% scale as the game.
Seal it with a timestamp: commit it, or send it to yourself in a message you won't edit. **You
will open it again in hour 4**, when your review shows whether the question has already been
answered.
If you use the [agent kit](https://github.com/Liberation-Labs-THCoalition/multiverse-courses/tree/master/courses/vibe-research/agent-kit), seal it by adding it at the
bottom of `research/PREDICTIONS.md` and committing: that is the file the kit's hooks read, and its git
check refuses a commit that quietly rewrites a prediction you already committed.

**Then the harder half (14 min).** Each student names a **cheaper question that would have to be
answered first**, and then answers one more: **with the model access you have, could you get data
that tests your claim at all?** *Yes*, *no*, or *I cannot tell yet*. *No* and *I cannot tell yet*
are session-one successes, not failures: you found out now rather than a month in.

*The claims, their sources and the "I was wrong" case are kept out of this public repository,
because the game only works once.*

## Hour 3 — Trace a number to its artifact

Covers `vr.verify-number` 0–6. **This is the centrepiece and it is entirely hands-on.** 50 minutes.

**Cards out (3 min).** Each student receives a **case card**: one sentence containing a number or a citation, exactly as it
appeared in print, and a pointer to where it came from. The job is to follow the pointer **all the
way to the artifact**, meaning the table, the dataset, the court record, or the edit that first
introduced it, and to write down what is actually there.

Every card is a real defect in real published work, and **most of them are still wrong at the first
place you will look.** Nothing in this hour is hypothetical.

**Two rounds of 13 minutes, one card each, swapped between rounds,** so that every card is traced by
two people who have not compared notes. Agents are allowed and expected. Note every time your agent
told you something was verified; the debrief uses it.

**The record sheet** has five lines per card:

1. the claim, as printed
2. what I actually opened (the URL or file, not the citation)
3. what it says, quoted
4. my verdict (*matches*, *disagrees*, *missing*, *impossible*, *superseded*, or *can't tell*) and
   how sure I am
5. **what I would have concluded if I had stopped one step earlier**

Line five is the one that teaches.

**Compare across the room (15 min).** First pair the two tracers of each card: where did your
verdicts differ, and who went one step further? Then sort every card on a shared board by *what
kind of wrong it was*, and **name the piles together**. Last, one question for everyone: **which
cards did anyone mark *matches*?**

**Close (6 min).** The card that fooled you, or nearly did, is the raw material for your first kill
entry in hour 4.

*The cards are kept out of this public repository too, for the same reason as hour 2's claims.*

## Hour 4 — Build the lit review, and get two things out of it

Covers the lit-review standard and feeds `vr.bound`. **50 minutes, then 11 more for the kill entry,
straight on.**

**Students build the review loop; we do not hand them one.** Twenty minutes, their own agent,
the supplied question. It does not need to be good — the **loop** needs to be theirs, because the
next part only works if they wrote the termination clause themselves.

The minimum loop: a search, a log of what came back, a rule for what counts as relevant, and **a
condition under which it stops**. That is four lines of scaffolding and one hard decision.

> **You cannot write a loop without a termination condition.** You already know this — you have
> known it since your first `while`. A question with no stopping condition is the same bug, in a
> shape no linter will ever catch for you.

### Job one — the review tells you where the edge is

Run it and answer two things in writing:

1. **Has this already been answered?** If yes, that is not a defeat, it is the fastest possible
   result. The endpoint arrived in one hour instead of six weeks. Take the next question.
2. **When did new sources stop changing the picture?** Write the number down.

That second one is **saturation**, and it is the first real stopping condition most people ever
write. Your agent will find papers forever — that is what it is for, and it is not a flaw. It has
no opinion about when you have enough. **Saturation is the opinion.** Without it you are not
reviewing a literature, you are being fed one.

**One swing (3 min).** Look at the saturated set and ask what every paper in it assumes. Write the
claim that would be true if that assumption were wrong, as a sixth line under your one-page. Make it
as big as you like: it is parked, not tested, and it is yours to take further, to the accelerator
if you want. Questions found this way tend to be more interesting than the gaps a review points at,
and gap-spotting is an agent's native mode, so this line has to come from you. (The method has a
name, problematization; [Tools by stage](tools-by-stage.md), stage 1, has more.)

**Now open the prediction you sealed in hour 2.** If the review settled the question, score your
prediction with the game's rule. If it did not, write down what would settle it.

*A review also shows you the reachable version of your question. Writing that down is part of the
homework.*

### Job two — the review is also where your first kill comes from

**Trace three citations your agent gave you, all the way to the source.** Exactly three: that is a
stopping rule too. If one of them does not check out (not "might be wrong", but traced and not
confirmed), that is the one you work with. If all three check out, work with the card from hour 3
that fooled you, or nearly did.

Then the move that makes the course work:

1. Write down what happened, with the artifact that revealed it
2. Generalise it from an instance to a **class** — name the class
3. Write it as a check someone else could apply without your context

Add it to your kill list. If you use the agent kit, that is `research/KILL_LIST.md`: its session-start
hook puts the whole list in front of your agent at the start of every session.

**That is their first kill entry, and they earned it in the first four hours.** A kill list you're
handed is a checklist. A kill list you earned is a memory — the entry means something because you
were there when it cost you.

**Last question of the day:** this hour gave you a boundary and a kill. Which one changed what you
are going to do next?

## Between sessions

*`DRAFT`, 2026-10-14.*

Between sessions, use your own agent as a **sparring partner**. Its job is to challenge your claim
and ask what would change your mind. It never names a failure mode for you: when you find one, you
name it and write the entry. Paste this in at the start of a conversation:

```text
I'm taking a research course, and between classes I'd like you to be my sparring partner.

I'll paste a claim, a design or a result. Challenge it, one probe at a time:
- Ask me one question that could break it. For example: What else could explain this?
  What result would change my mind? What does the source actually say, next to what I wrote?
  Did that search really run, and could it have found something if it was there?
- Sometimes ask the other way: what result would show I'm right, and could I actually get it?
- Then wait for my answer before you ask the next one.

Two rules:
- Never name a failure mode for me, or give me a label for what went wrong. If we find a
  problem, I name it and write it down myself. You may tell me whether my name would make
  sense to someone who wasn't here.
- Don't fix things for me. Ask the next question instead.

Be warm and direct. This is practice, not a test.

After each probe, write one record in exactly this form:

PROBE <number>
Challenged: <what you questioned, in one line>
I answered: <my answer, in my own words>
Survived: <what is still standing: all of it, part of it, or none of it>

When I say "done", list every record together so I can save them.
```

Save the records with your one-page. They show what you tested and what held up.

**Homework:**

1. **The reachable version of your question.** Your review in hour 4 showed where other people
   stopped, and usually why. That boundary is the shape of a claim you can actually close: write
   it down.
2. **Three sources for your claim**, each either verified to a primary or explicitly marked
   *unverifiable, and why*. The second category is a passing answer.
3. **A two-condition design for session 2** (about 15 minutes, once its target is posted). Session 2's
   target is posted about five days before the session. Write a line or two each for the claim it tests,
   the two conditions, the items and the measurement, as
   [Before session 2](session-2-designing-to-fail.md#before-session-2) sets out. A rough draft is enough,
   and don't run anything yet: session 2's hour 1 is built to take it apart.
