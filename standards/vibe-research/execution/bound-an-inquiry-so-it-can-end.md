# Bound an inquiry so it can end

slug: vr.bound

## Description
A claim can be perfectly falsifiable and still have no end. Nothing about it is wrong, no
review gate will ever fire on it, and you can work for a month with every individual step
defensible and arrive nowhere. This standard covers the skill that prevents that: writing
down, before you start, what result — in either direction — would let you stop, and then
either honouring it or revising it out loud with a stated reason.

The failure this guards against is not error. It is **drift**, and it is produced most
reliably by a good collaborator rather than a bad one. An agent can always propose a next
step, and the next step is usually sensible: *control for that first*, *it would be stronger
with another condition*, *before we can answer this we need to establish…* Each is good
advice. In sequence and unbounded, they are a goose chase with excellent manners.

You cannot out-argue this in the moment, because in the moment the suggestion is genuinely
reasonable and you are genuinely curious. The only defence is a stopping condition authored
by the version of you who was not yet invested.

**This transfers directly from your prerequisites.** When you build an automation you write
the acceptance test first — you know what "working" looks like before you write a line. This
is the same muscle pointed at a question. Almost nobody writes an acceptance test for a
question, which is why almost everybody's first research project has no end.

A question with no stopping condition is not a question. It is a topic.

---
## Assessment Requirements
In order to fully cover this standard, a performance must be recording for each of the following objectives:

0. State, before any data is collected, what result would let you stop — in **both**
   directions, not only the confirming one
1. State the cost you are willing to spend before the stopping condition is reviewed, in a
   unit you actually track (hours, runs, compute, or sessions)
2. Keep a log of next-step suggestions from your agent or collaborators, and mark each one
   as **toward** the endpoint or **extending** it
3. Identify at least one suggestion you declined, and state what it would have cost to follow
4. Revise a stopping condition at least once, in writing, with the reason stated **before**
   the new result is known — distinguishing a revision from a rationalisation
5. Report an inquiry you stopped, and say whether you stopped because it ended or because it
   ran out of budget; both are legitimate and they are different outcomes

---
## Notes for the facilitator

**Objective 2 is the one that does the work**, and it is cheap: a two-column log kept live
during the session. Students are routinely surprised by the ratio. The point is not that
extending suggestions are bad — many are correct — but that the ratio is invisible until it
is written down, and an unlogged sequence of reasonable extensions is indistinguishable from
progress while you are inside it.

**Objective 4 is the hardest to assess honestly.** A revision made before seeing the result is
a design decision. The identical revision made after is a rationalisation. The only thing
separating them is the timestamp, which is why it must be written rather than remembered.

**Objective 5 must not be graded on which answer was given.** Running out of budget is a real
and respectable end. A student who says *"I stopped because I had spent the four sessions and
the question was still open"* has met this standard completely. A student who cannot say which
one happened has not.

**Do not let this become a productivity standard.** It is not about working faster or scoping
smaller. Ambition is fine and big swings are the point — a bounded inquiry can be enormous.
What it cannot be is *open*. The test is whether an outcome exists that would end it, not
whether that outcome is near.
